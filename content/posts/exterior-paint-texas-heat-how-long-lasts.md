# Why Exterior Paint Fails Fast in Texas — and How Long It Should Actually Last

Exterior paint is sold on a warranty measured in decades. In Central Texas you should plan on considerably less than the box says, and you should expect one or two sides of your house to give up long before the others.

That second part is the useful bit, because it means the right job is often not a whole-house repaint.

---

## Realistic lifespans here

| Surface | Typical Central Texas life |
|---|---|
| Fiber cement siding (Hardie), factory finish | 10 – 15 years |
| Fiber cement, field painted | 7 – 12 years |
| Stucco | 7 – 12 years |
| Wood siding | 4 – 7 years |
| Wood trim and fascia | 3 – 6 years |
| Front door, west-facing | 2 – 4 years |
| Painted brick | 8 – 15 years |
| Shutters and accents | 3 – 6 years |

Compare with the ten to fifteen years a premium exterior paint advertises. Those numbers come from moderate climates. Ours is not one.

**And the split matters more than the average.** North-facing walls routinely outlast south and west walls by five years or more on the same house with the same paint applied the same day.

---

## What actually destroys it

### Ultraviolet light

The main killer. UV breaks down the resin binder holding the pigment together. As the binder degrades, pigment is released as a fine powder — run your hand down a sun-beaten wall and if it comes away coloured, that is **chalking**, and it is the binder failing.

Central Texas gets intense sun for a long season. South and west elevations take the worst of it, which is why they fail first and why deep, saturated colours fade fastest — dark pigments absorb more energy and run hotter.

### Heat and thermal cycling

A dark west-facing wall can reach well over 150°F on an August afternoon and drop 60 or 70 degrees overnight. Every cycle expands and contracts both the substrate and the paint film. Do that a few thousand times and the film loses flexibility, then cracks.

This is why **caulk joints fail before the paint does** — they move the most.

### Moisture from behind

Counterintuitive but common: paint usually peels because of water getting behind it, not water hitting the front.

Sources here are predictable — failed caulk at trim and windows, sprinklers hitting the wall daily, gutters overflowing, unsealed cut ends on fiber cement, and bare wood at fascia and rake boards.

**If paint is peeling in sheets rather than flaking, suspect water from behind.** Repainting over it will fail in the same place within a year.

### Hard water and mineral deposits

Central Texas water is hard. Sprinkler spray hitting a wall leaves mineral scale that breaks the film down and leaves white staining. A sprinkler head aimed at the house is a slow paint stripper. Check yours — it is the cheapest exterior paint life extension available.

### Mildew on the north side

The side that never dries fully grows mildew, which shows as grey-black speckling. It is not paint failure, it is biological growth on the surface, and it needs to be killed and washed off before repainting or it grows straight back through the new coat.

## Reading your own house

Walk the perimeter and look for:

**Chalking** — powder on your hand. The binder has broken down. Needs a thorough wash and often a bonding primer.

**Fading** — compare a sheltered spot under an eave with open wall. The gap tells you how much UV damage has accumulated.

**Alligatoring** — a scaly cracked pattern like reptile skin. Usually too many coats built up over years, or a hard coat over a soft one. Needs scraping, not overcoating.

**Peeling in sheets** — moisture behind the film. Find the source first.

**Flaking in small chips** — normal end-of-life UV degradation.

**Failed caulk** — open joints at trim, windows, corner boards. Often the only thing that actually needs doing.

**Exposed wood** — bare fascia, rake boards, window sills. Untreated wood in this climate goes fast. This is urgent in a way that faded siding is not.

## You may not need to paint the whole house

The default quote is a whole-house repaint. Sometimes that is right. Often it is not.

**Paint just the failing elevations.** If south and west are shot and north and east look fine, do the two that need it. Colour match is imperfect on an aged wall, but the corner of a house is a natural break line and most people never notice.

**Paint just the trim.** Trim and fascia fail two to three times faster than siding. A trim-only repaint is a fraction of a full job and buys the siding several more years, while fixing the part where water actually gets in.

**Caulk and spot-prime instead.** If the film is sound but joints have opened, re-caulking and spot-priming bare areas is maintenance, not a repaint, and it can add years.

A contractor who will quote you the smaller job is worth keeping.

## What makes an exterior job last here

**Wash first, properly.** Chalk, dirt and mildew have to come off or the new coat bonds to loose powder. Mildew needs a cleaner that kills it, not just pressure.

**Scrape and feather every failing edge.** Whatever is loose comes off. A hard edge left behind telegraphs through and starts the next failure.

**Prime bare substrate.** Bare wood, bare fiber cement, exposed masonry, rusty nail heads — all need the right primer. Over chalking, a bonding primer is not optional.

**Caulk the right joints — and not the wrong ones.** Vertical trim joints, window and door surrounds, corner boards, penetrations: yes. The bottom horizontal edge of siding laps: no. Those are drainage paths, and sealing them traps water inside the wall.

**Two coats.** One-coat exteriors in this sun are a two-thirds-life job.

**Watch the weather.** Do not paint surfaces above roughly 90°F or in direct afternoon sun in July — the film flashes off before it can level or bond. Good crews chase shade around the house through the day and start early. **Spring and autumn are the right seasons here.**

**Aim sprinklers away from the house.** Free, and it matters more than the paint you choose.

## Colour, honestly

Deep saturated colours — navy, charcoal, forest, barn red — look excellent and fade fastest, because they absorb more energy and their pigments are more UV-vulnerable.

If you want a dark exterior in Central Texas, it is a legitimate choice; just budget for a shorter repaint cycle, especially on south and west walls. Lighter and mid-tone colours stay looking right considerably longer.

Ask about products formulated for high-UV regions. The difference is real in this climate.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should I paint my house exterior in Texas?**
Fiber cement siding every 8 to 12 years, stucco 7 to 12, wood siding 4 to 7, and trim and fascia 3 to 6. South and west elevations need attention years before north and east, so a partial repaint is often the right call.

**Why is my exterior paint peeling after only two years?**
Two-year failure is not normal wear — it is almost always a bond problem or moisture from behind. Common causes: painted over chalk without washing, no primer on bare wood or fiber cement, sprinklers hitting the wall, failed caulk letting water in, or painted in direct sun on a very hot surface.

**What is chalking and does it mean I need to repaint?**
Chalking is the paint binder breaking down under UV, releasing pigment as powder. Light chalking is normal ageing and can be washed and painted over with a bonding primer. Heavy chalking means the film is near end of life.

**Can I paint just one side of my house?**
Yes, and in Texas it is often the sensible choice, since sun exposure makes elevations fail at very different rates. Colour match on aged paint will not be perfect, but corners give a natural break. Ask for the partial quote.

**What is the best time of year to paint an exterior in Central Texas?**
Spring and autumn. Mild temperatures, low humidity, less intense sun. Summer is workable early in the day in shade, but surface temperatures over about 90°F cause the film to dry before it bonds properly.

**Should I pressure wash before painting?**
The surface must be clean, but high pressure on wood or fiber cement can drive water into the substrate and damage the surface. Lower pressure with the right cleaner, worked properly, beats blasting it. On mildew, the cleaner matters more than the pressure.

**Does painting brick require anything different?**
Yes — and it is close to irreversible, so think carefully. Brick must be clean, fully dry, and primed with a masonry primer. Use a breathable masonry paint; trapping moisture in brick causes spalling. Once painted, brick needs repainting permanently.

## The summary

Texas sun shortens exterior paint life well below what the can promises, and it does it unevenly — south and west go first.

Most failures here are not worn-out paint. They are water getting behind the film through failed caulk, bare wood or a sprinkler pointed the wrong way. Fix the cause, then paint.

And before agreeing to a whole-house repaint, ask whether two elevations and the trim would do.
