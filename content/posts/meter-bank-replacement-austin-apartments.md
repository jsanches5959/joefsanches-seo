# Meter Bank Replacement for Older Austin Apartments: A Property Manager's Guide

If you manage an older apartment building in the Austin area, there is a decent chance you have already had some version of this conversation: an inspector, an insurance underwriter, or the utility looked at your meter bank and used the word "replace" where you were hoping to hear "repair."

This is the guide we would want in your position — why this is landing on older buildings now, what the job actually involves, what it typically costs, and the part nobody budgets for, which is keeping tenants in power while their building's electrical service is rebuilt.

## Why this is happening now

Three things converged on the same piece of equipment:

**The code changed.** The 2020 National Electrical Code (section 230.85) requires an exterior emergency disconnect on dwelling services, and as cities across the metro adopted that cycle, rebuilt services have to have one. An old meter bank has nowhere to put it. Once a service is being rebuilt rather than simply repaired, it has to come up to the current standard — which usually means the whole assembly, not the one socket that failed.

**The equipment aged out.** Much of the multi-family stock built around Austin in the seventies and eighties is running meter sockets that are corroded, heat-damaged, or from manufacturers whose replacement parts no longer exist. A burned socket in a bank with no available parts is not a repair. It's a replacement you schedule or a replacement the fire marshal schedules.

**Insurance and transactions started asking.** Underwriters and buyers' inspectors both photograph meter banks now. A flagged bank can stall a renewal, a sale, or a refinance — which turns an electrical job into a deadline with money attached.

## The short answer on cost

| Scope | Typical price |
|---|---|
| Single meter socket replacement (parts still available) | $900 – $1,800 |
| Multi-meter bank rebuild, priced per meter position | $1,200 – $2,800 |
| Full 3–6 position bank with new disconnects and trough | $9,000 – $25,000 |
| Service size increase or new mast/main, added to the above | Quoted after survey |

These are ranges for planning, and meter work varies more than most — service size, how the building is fed, and how much of the existing assembly can stay all move the number. Every building gets surveyed before a written quote. Anyone who prices a meter bank over the phone is guessing with your money.

## What the job actually looks like

We rebuilt a three-unit meter stack on a multi-family building in Central Texas — it's on our work page, before and after. Gutter trough across the top, three meter sockets and three emergency disconnects below, every conduit run plumb, every label legible, all fed from a single service point. That job is typical, and its sequence is the part worth understanding:

1. **Survey.** What's actually on the wall, what the utility's records say is there, and where they disagree — they often disagree. Panel locations in every unit, and what each tenant has that cannot lose power.
2. **Utility coordination.** Inside Austin that's **Austin Energy**; in Leander, Cedar Park and much of the surrounding metro it's **Pedernales Electric**. The utility disconnects and reconnects, and their schedule — not the crew's — sets the outage window. This has to be booked before a date is promised to anyone.
3. **Permit.** Pulled by the licensed electrical contractor performing the work.
4. **Tenant notice and scheduling.** Written notice ahead of the outage, a defined window, and a plan for the tenants with medical equipment, home businesses, or nowhere to go. This step is skipped constantly and is the one tenants remember.
5. **The swap.** Old assembly off, new trough, sockets and disconnects on, services re-landed. On a small bank this is one long day.
6. **Inspection, then reconnect.** The city inspects; the utility reconnects after approval. Sequenced well, tenants lose power for hours. Sequenced badly, they lose it overnight — and the difference is planning, not luck.

## Patch or replace: the honest version

**Patching makes sense when** one socket has failed in a bank that's otherwise sound, parts are available, and nobody — utility, inspector, insurer — has flagged the assembly. A single socket replacement is a real job at a fair price, and we'll tell you if that's all you have.

**Replacement is the answer when** two or more of these are true:

- The bank's manufacturer or socket style is obsolete — parts are used-market only
- There's heat damage, corrosion or arcing marks beyond the one failed position
- The building is being sold, refinanced or re-insured and the bank has been flagged
- The utility has tagged the service
- You have paid for the same bank twice in three years

The arithmetic that settles it for most owners: a rebuild is one outage, one permit, one inspection, and a 30-year reset. Three separate emergency patches cost more in total, each one comes with its own outage and its own after-hours call, and at the end of them you still own an old bank.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long will my tenants be without power?**
On a small bank, hours — a single planned outage inside one working day, if the utility window and the inspection are sequenced properly. Larger banks or a service size increase can take two days. What tenants are owed either way is a specific window in writing, in advance, not a surprise.

**Who coordinates with Austin Energy or Pedernales Electric?**
We do, as part of managing the job. The utility disconnect/reconnect is the long-lead item — it gets booked first and the tenant notice goes out against a confirmed date, not a hoped-for one.

**Who pulls the permit?**
The licensed electrical contractor performing the work. That's not a formality: the permit, the inspection and the liability all sit with the license holder, which is exactly why the work belongs to them and the management of it belongs to us.

**Can one bad meter socket just be replaced?**
Sometimes — see the patch-or-replace section above. The deciding factors are parts availability and the condition of the rest of the bank. We will tell you honestly which one you have, because a socket replacement we talk you out of costs us a job today and earns us the building.

**What triggers this — do I have to wait to be flagged?**
No. The cheapest version of this job is the planned one: surveyed, quoted, scheduled around your occupancy, done in one outage. The expensive version is the emergency one, after a failure, with a red tag on the wall and a utility deadline. If your bank is over thirty years old, a survey now is worth more than an opinion later.

**Do you handle the units' interior panels too?**
If a unit panel needs replacing as part of the same project — older panels with known problems often surface during the survey — it goes on the same scope and the same outage window, so tenants are dark once, not twice.
