# Orange Peel vs Knockdown: Why Your Drywall Patch Still Shows

You filled the hole. You sanded it flat. You painted it. And you can still see exactly where it was, especially in the afternoon when light comes across the wall.

This is the most common complaint in drywall work, and the cause is almost never the compound. It is one of two things:

1. **The texture does not match.**
2. **The sheen does not match.**

Both are fixable. Neither is fixed by more mud.

---

## First, identify what you actually have

Stand at the wall, then look at it from an angle with a light source raking across it. Texture is invisible head-on and obvious from the side.

### Orange peel (also called splatter)

Fine bumps, like the skin of an orange. Sprayed on wet and left alone — never flattened. This is the default in most Central Texas production homes built from the 1990s onward. If you live in a Leander or Cedar Park subdivision built in the last thirty years, this is probably you.

Varies a lot in coarseness. A fine orange peel and a heavy one look like different textures entirely, and matching means matching the **droplet size**, not just the concept.

### Knockdown

Sprayed on heavier than orange peel, left to set for a few minutes, then flattened with a wide blade. The result is mottled flat islands with texture between them — like stucco that has been pressed down.

The look depends almost entirely on **timing**. Knock it down too early and it smears into mush; too late and it stays as unflattened lumps. The window is a few minutes and it changes with humidity, which is why the same technique produces different results in February and August.

### Smooth / Level 5

No texture at all. A skim coat over the entire surface, sanded flat.

Counterintuitively, this is the **hardest** finish to repair invisibly. There is no pattern to hide the transition, so any difference in flatness shows as a shadow. Level 5 repairs are usually priced higher than textured ones for exactly this reason.

### Skip trowel / Santa Fe / hand textures

Applied by hand with a trowel, producing broad flat swaths with irregular edges. Popular in Hill Country and Spanish-style homes.

These are the hardest to match because you are matching **a specific person's hand movement** from years ago. Two experienced finishers produce visibly different skip trowel. Honest expectation: a good match, not an identical one.

### Popcorn / acoustic ceilings

Sprayed aggregate, common from the 1960s through the 1990s. Repairs almost never blend convincingly — the aggregate size and density are basically impossible to replicate in a patch.

The usual honest recommendation for a damaged popcorn ceiling is to scrape the whole ceiling and refinish it smooth or in a light knockdown. **If the house predates the mid-1980s, test for asbestos before scraping anything.** Test kits are inexpensive; the alternative is expensive.

## Why texture matching is the actual skill

Anyone can fill a hole. The reason a professional repair disappears comes down to things that are not obvious:

**Spray, do not brush.** Orange peel and knockdown are sprayed with a hopper gun. People try to replicate it by stippling with a brush or a rolled-on product, and it reads wrong — too regular, too uniform. Real spray texture is random in a way a hand cannot fake.

**Thin the mud correctly.** All-purpose joint compound thinned to roughly the consistency of thick paint or pancake batter. Too thick and you get heavy blobs; too thin and it runs. This is adjusted by feel on the day.

**Set the air pressure for the droplet size.** Higher pressure with a smaller nozzle gives finer droplets. Lower pressure and a wider nozzle gives coarser splatter. This is the dial that actually controls the match.

**Test on cardboard first.** Every time. Spray a scrap, hold it up next to the wall, adjust, respray. Nobody gets it first try, and the people who look like they do are testing off-camera.

**Feather the mud wide before texturing.** A patch feathered eight inches leaves a visible plateau under the texture. Sixteen inches or more, so the transition is below the threshold your eye can catch.

**Match the coarseness, not just the type.** "Orange peel" is not one texture. Getting the category right and the grade wrong still leaves a visible patch.

## The half everyone forgets: sheen

Here is the failure that sends people back to the wall a second time.

You matched the texture perfectly. You painted the patch with paint from the same can. And there is still a visible rectangle.

That is **sheen flashing**, and it happens because:

- **New compound is porous.** It drinks paint. The surrounding wall, painted years ago, does not. The same paint dries to a different lustre on the two surfaces.
- **Old paint has changed.** Sunlight, cleaning and time dull a wall's sheen. A fresh coat of the identical product is shinier than its neighbours.
- **Higher sheens punish you harder.** Flat forgives almost everything. Eggshell forgives some. Satin and semi-gloss forgive nothing — they reflect light directionally, which advertises every difference in surface. See our [paint sheen guide](/posts/paint-sheen-guide-eggshell-satin-semigloss).

**The fix, in order:**

1. **Prime the patch** with a quality primer, ideally one made to seal repairs. This equalises porosity. Skipping this is the number one cause of a visible repair.
2. **Paint the entire wall, corner to corner.** Stop at a real edge — an inside corner, a door casing, a ceiling line. Do not stop in the middle of a wall, because the stopping point becomes the new visible line.
3. **On a high-sheen wall, consider doing the whole room.** Satin and semi-gloss carry sheen differences around corners more than you would expect.

## The realistic repair sequence

1. Cut the damage back to something square and sound.
2. Back the hole — a scrap of drywall or a backer strip behind the opening, so the patch has something to bear on.
3. Set the patch flush, and tape the joints.
4. Three coats of compound, each feathered wider than the last. Dry fully between coats; this is where the days go.
5. Sand with a light held at an angle so you can see what you are doing.
6. **Test the texture on cardboard.** Adjust. Test again.
7. Spray the texture. If it is knockdown, wait and knock it down at the right moment.
8. Let it dry, then prime.
9. Paint the full wall.

Steps 6 and 8 are the ones that get skipped, and they are the two that decide whether the repair is invisible.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between orange peel and knockdown texture?**
Both are sprayed. Orange peel is left as sprayed, giving fine even bumps. Knockdown is sprayed heavier, allowed to set a few minutes, then flattened with a blade, giving mottled flat patches. Knockdown is the more dramatic, larger-scale pattern; orange peel is subtle and uniform.

**Can I match drywall texture without a sprayer?**
For very small patches you can sometimes approximate fine orange peel with a stipple sponge or a textured roller, and aerosol texture cans exist for exactly this. Results are mixed and get worse as the patch gets larger. For anything bigger than a fist, or on a wall in good light, a hopper gun is the difference between a repair and a visible scar.

**Why does my patch look fine until the afternoon?**
Raking light. When the sun comes low through a window it crosses the wall at a shallow angle and exaggerates any difference in flatness or sheen. Always inspect a repair with a work light held flat against the wall — if it passes that, it passes daylight.

**How wide should I feather a drywall patch?**
Wider than you think. Final coat 12 to 16 inches beyond the patch edge for a typical repair, more on smooth walls. Narrow feathering is the most common amateur mistake and creates the low-relief "bump" you can see from across the room.

**Should I texture over the whole wall instead of matching?**
Sometimes that is the right call. If the existing texture is a hand pattern, unusually heavy, or you have several repairs on one wall, retexturing the entire wall gives a guaranteed uniform result. It costs more up front and removes the risk of a mismatch.

**Do I have to paint the whole wall after a repair?**
Effectively yes, if you want the repair to be invisible. Prime the patch, then paint corner to corner. The only common exception is a fresh flat-painted wall under a few years old where you still have the original paint.

**Is smooth wall really harder to repair than textured?**
Yes. Texture is forgiving — the pattern breaks up your eye and hides small transitions. A smooth Level 5 wall gives your eye a perfectly even surface to compare against, so any deviation reads instantly. Smooth repairs need wider feathering and more careful sanding.

## The summary

A patch shows for two reasons, and neither is the mud: the texture is wrong, or the sheen is wrong.

Match the texture by spraying, not brushing, and test on cardboard first. Match the sheen by priming the patch and painting the wall corner to corner.

Get both right and the repair disappears — even in the afternoon light.
