# Nail Pops in a Two-Year-Old House: Why It Happens and What Actually Fixes It

You bought new. Two years in, small round bumps are appearing on the walls and ceilings, often in neat vertical rows. Some have popped the paint into a little circle of flaking.

This is not a defect, and it is not your foundation. It is lumber drying out, and in Central Texas new construction it is close to universal.

Here is what is happening, why the obvious fix makes it worse, and how to get your builder to pay for it.

---

## What a nail pop actually is

Drywall is fastened to the wood framing with nails or screws, then the head is covered with joint compound and painted. The wall you see is a thin skin of gypsum over a wooden skeleton.

Framing lumber arrives on site wetter than it will ever be again. Kiln-dried studs typically ship around 19 percent moisture content. Once your house is closed up and the HVAC is running, that lumber equalises to something nearer 8 to 12 percent — and in a Central Texas summer with the air conditioning on, often lower.

Wood shrinks as it dries. Not much along its length, but noticeably across its width.

As the stud shrinks away from the back of the drywall, the fastener — which is anchored in the wood — travels with it. The drywall stays put, held by the fasteners around it. The result is a fastener head that is now proud of the drywall surface, pushing the compound and paint outward into a small dome.

Then someone leans on the wall, or the door slams, and the compound cracks and flakes off the top.

**This is why year two is the peak.** The first summer is the big drying event, and the movement shows up over the following months.

---

## Why it is so common in Leander and Cedar Park specifically

Three things compound here:

**Fast build cycles.** Lumber that sat in a rainy yard and went up in a wet month starts wetter. A fast-growing market builds fast.

**Extreme humidity swing.** Central Texas runs from very humid to very dry across a year. The delta between a muggy June and a bone-dry, air-conditioned August is large, and lumber moves with it.

**Nails rather than screws.** Screws hold far better against this kind of withdrawal because the threads bite. Nails are quicker to drive and some production framing still uses them on ceilings and walls. Nailed drywall pops more.

None of this means your house was built badly. It means your house was built out of wood.

## The fix that makes it worse

The instinct is to hammer the nail back in and mud over it.

Do not do that. The nail is still in the same shrinking stud. You have re-seated it temporarily, the wood is still moving, and in another season you have the same pop in exactly the same place — plus a crushed, weakened area of drywall face around it from the hammer.

**What actually works:**

1. **Drive a drywall screw about an inch and a half above or below the popped fastener**, into the same stud. Set it just below the paper surface — dimpled, not torn through. This is the fastener that will hold the sheet from now on.
2. **Deal with the original.** Either drive the old nail well below the surface, or remove it if it comes cleanly. Do not leave it proud.
3. **Scrape off the loose compound and flaking paint** back to a sound edge.
4. **Three thin coats of compound**, each feathered wider — final pass 8 to 12 inches across. Thin coats. A single thick blob shrinks as it dries and cracks.
5. **Sand with light held at an angle** so you can see the transition.
6. **Match the texture.** Most Central Texas builds are orange peel — sprayed, not brushed. See [matching drywall texture](/posts/match-drywall-texture-orange-peel-knockdown).
7. **Prime the repairs, then paint wall to wall.**

The screw is the actual repair. Everything after it is cosmetics.

## Use your builder warranty first

This is the part worth money to you, and most people miss the window.

Nearly every production builder in Central Texas offers a **one-year fit-and-finish warranty** covering exactly this: drywall cracks, nail pops, and a touch-up visit. Many schedule an eleven-month walkthrough specifically so you can list defects before the one-year mark.

Typical structure — check your own documents, as it varies by builder:

- **Year 1:** workmanship and finishes, including drywall and paint touch-up
- **Years 1–2:** systems — plumbing, electrical, HVAC
- **Years 1–10:** structural

**What to do:**

1. **Find your warranty documents** and the exact expiry date.
2. **Walk the house with a flashlight held flat against the wall.** Raking light shows every pop. Do this before you call, not during the visit.
3. **Photograph and list everything**, room by room. One organised list gets taken seriously; three phone calls over three months gets you one visit.
4. **Submit in writing** before the deadline, even if scheduling happens later. The written submission date is what counts.
5. **Expect one visit.** Builders typically do a single drywall and paint touch-up under the fit-and-finish warranty. Save it for when the movement has mostly finished — late in year one, not month two.

If you are past the window, this is a normal paid repair and not an expensive one.

## When a "nail pop" is not a nail pop

Most are harmless. A few things are worth a second look:

- **Rows of pops along a ceiling with a sagging line** can indicate a truss or joist issue rather than simple shrinkage.
- **Pops accompanied by wide or tapering wall cracks, or doors that stopped closing**, point at movement rather than drying. See [which drywall cracks mean foundation trouble](/posts/drywall-cracks-when-to-worry-central-texas).
- **A brown or yellow ring around the pop** is water, not shrinkage. Find the source before patching anything — see [water-damaged drywall](/posts/water-damaged-drywall-patch-or-replace).
- **Pops still appearing heavily in year five or later** are unusual. Normal shrinkage has finished by then.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are nail pops a structural problem?**
Almost never. A nail pop is the fastener moving with drying lumber, which is a cosmetic consequence of normal wood behaviour. Structural problems show up as wide cracks, tapering cracks, stair-step cracking in exterior masonry, and doors and windows that stop operating.

**Will nail pops keep coming back?**
Not if they are fixed properly. The repair that lasts adds a screw into the same stud a short distance away, so the sheet is held by a fastener that bites. Re-driving the original nail and mudding over it fails again within a season or two.

**How many nail pops are normal in a new house?**
A handful to a few dozen through the first two years is ordinary in Central Texas production housing. Ceilings show more than walls. It is one of the most common items on an eleven-month warranty list.

**Should I fix nail pops before painting?**
Yes, and before selling. They are cheap to correct while the painter is already there, and a wall full of small bumps reads as neglect to a buyer or an inspector even though the cause is harmless.

**Can I fix nail pops myself?**
The mechanics are simple — screw, scrape, mud, sand. The part that defeats most DIY attempts is matching sprayed texture and avoiding sheen flash afterwards. On a flat-painted wall you plan to repaint, it is a reasonable weekend job. On a textured wall in a well-lit room, the repair often ends up more visible than the pop.

**Do screws prevent nail pops entirely?**
They reduce them substantially but do not eliminate them. Screws resist withdrawal far better than nails because the threads hold in the wood, but enough shrinkage will still move a fastener. Screws are why modern drywall work pops less than older nailed work.
