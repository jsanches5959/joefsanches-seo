# What EV Charger Installation Actually Costs in Leander & Cedar Park (2026 Numbers)

Search "EV charger installation cost" and you get the price of the charger. That is the smaller half of the bill, and it is the half the ads want you looking at.

The charger itself is a box with a cable. The installation is a new 240-volt circuit run from your panel to wherever the car sleeps, sized and protected correctly, permitted, and inspected. That is where the money and the variance are, and it is why one neighbour paid $700 all-in and the next one paid $3,000 for what sounds like the same job.

Here is what it actually costs in Leander, Cedar Park, Georgetown and greater Austin, and what moves the number.

## Why the range is so wide

### The panel decides most of it

Every EV charger install starts with a load calculation: what your electrical service can already carry, and what's left. This is where Leander and Cedar Park homeowners have an advantage — most of the housing stock here is new enough to have a 200-amp panel with room on it. If your panel has capacity and a spare breaker space, the charger is a straightforward new circuit.

The expensive version is an older or smaller panel. A 100-amp panel in an older Austin home, already carrying electric heat, a dryer and a range, may have nothing left to give. Then your choices are a panel upgrade or a load management device that pauses charging when the house is pulling hard. The device is usually the right answer — it costs a few hundred dollars and does the same job for this purpose. Anyone who quotes you a panel upgrade without mentioning load management is selling you the bigger ticket.

### Distance is the second driver

Electricians charge for the run. Panel in the garage, charger on the wall next to it: short run, heavier-gauge wire for a few feet, done in a couple of hours. Panel on the opposite side of the house, or in a detached building: the wire alone — copper, thick enough for 40 to 60 amps — gets expensive, and fishing it through attics and walls is slow work. Measure the distance from your panel to where the car parks before you call anyone. That number predicts your quote better than anything else.

### Hardwired or plug-in

A hardwired charger connects directly to the circuit. A plug-in charger uses a NEMA 14-50 outlet, which current code requires to be on a GFCI breaker — a breaker that costs noticeably more than a standard one. Hardwired is usually the better install: full 48-amp charging instead of 40, no expensive breaker, one less connection to fail. Plug-in makes sense if you want to take the charger when you move.

### Amperage: what you're actually buying

A 60-amp circuit delivers 48 amps to the car, roughly 40-plus miles of range per hour of charging. A 40-amp circuit delivers 32 — high twenties per hour. For a car that sits in the garage ten hours a night, both are full by morning. Don't pay for the bigger circuit and the heavier wire unless your driving actually needs it.

## Who your utility is

Most of Leander and Cedar Park is **Pedernales Electric Cooperative** territory; inside Austin it's **Austin Energy**. A straightforward garage charger usually doesn't need the utility involved at all. A panel or service upgrade does — the utility has to agree the service drop to your house can carry it, and that coordination adds time, not usually much cost. Your installer should handle it; if they look blank when you mention it, that's information.

## What you can reasonably do yourself

Honest assessment, including the parts that argue against hiring anyone:

**Worth doing yourself:**
- Buying the charger. Prices are the same everywhere; there's no contractor discount worth routing it through us.
- Deciding where it goes. You know which side of the car the charge port is on and how the cable will drape. Walk it before the installer comes.
- Checking your panel for a "200" on the main breaker and spare slots, and photographing it. That photo is the single most useful thing you can send with an estimate request.

**Not worth doing yourself:**
- The circuit. In Texas, installing a new 240-volt circuit is licensed electrical work, and on a 60-amp run it is genuinely unforgiving of mistakes — a loose connection on a circuit that pulls full load for six hours straight is how garage fires start. This is not the job to learn on.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much does it cost to install a Tesla charger at home in Leander TX?**
For most newer Leander and Cedar Park homes — 200-amp panel, charger in the same garage — plan on $900 to $2,500 all-in, charger included. The Tesla Wall Connector itself is in the $400 to $550 range; the rest is the circuit, the breaker, the permit and the labour. A panel upgrade or a long run is what pushes a quote above that range.

**Do I need a panel upgrade to charge an EV?**
Usually not. Most homes here have 200-amp service with capacity to spare, and where capacity is tight, a load management device that throttles charging when the house is busy solves it for a few hundred dollars. A panel upgrade is the right answer when the panel is old, full, or a brand with a known failure history — not as a default.

**Do I need a permit to install an EV charger in Cedar Park or Leander?**
Yes — a new circuit is permitted electrical work in both cities, and the charger manufacturers' warranties generally assume a permitted install. The permit itself is a small part of the total. An installer who suggests skipping it is suggesting you carry the risk instead of him.

**How fast will my car charge?**
On a 60-amp circuit, about 40-plus miles of range per hour. On a 40-amp circuit, high twenties. Either one charges a typical EV from low to full overnight. The difference matters to rideshare drivers and almost nobody else.

**Can I install chargers for two EVs?**
Yes. Many chargers — Tesla's Wall Connector among them — can share one circuit and split the power between two cars automatically, which is much cheaper than two separate circuits. Tell the installer a second EV is coming before the first install, not after.

**How long does the install take?**
The work itself is usually half a day. Scheduling the permit inspection adds a day or two around it. A panel upgrade is a full day and involves a short utility-coordinated outage.
